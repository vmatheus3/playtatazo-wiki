The Naturalist's Field Course

Told by Dr. Fernwhistle the Heron, from level 10.

  1. Meet the naturalist: Dr. Fernwhistle's camp is on the Primeval Isle. Talk to her.

    Birds are living dinosaurs. The sparrow on a fence and the Velociraptor share ancestors, and scientists have found hundreds of dinosaur fossils with feathers.

  2. Study a Parasaurolophus: Get within 12 m of one without it chasing you (a Calm Aura helps) and watch for 5 seconds.

    Parasaurolophus's long crest was hollow, with tubes running from the nose. Blowing air through it probably made deep, low calls, a bit like a trombone, that could carry far across the plains.

  3. Bring three fossils: Mine fossil beds (and sometimes bone rocks) and bring 3 fossils to Dr. Fernwhistle.

    A fossil forms when an animal is buried quickly, in mud, sand or ash. Over millions of years, minerals in the water slowly replace the bone, turning it to stone in the same shape.

  4. Bring a piece of amber: Amber boulders on the isle hold it (mine them), and old trees drop it. Bring 1 to Dr. Fernwhistle.

    Amber is tree resin that hardened over millions of years. Tiny insects, feathers and even a dinosaur's tail have been found trapped inside, preserved in fine detail.

  5. Calm two Velociraptors: They hunt in pairs in the ferns. Calm (cleanse) two of them.

    Velociraptor was about the size of a turkey. Bumps on its arm bones, called quill knobs, are where big feathers were anchored, just as on the wing bones of birds today.

  6. Photograph a Pteranodon: Open photo mode (P) and take a picture with a Pteranodon in the frame.

    Pteranodon was not a dinosaur. It was a pterosaur, a flying reptile and a close cousin of the dinosaurs. Its wings were skin stretched from one hugely long fourth finger.

  7. Study a Triceratops: Watch a Triceratops for 5 seconds from within 12 m without it charging.

    Triceratops lived at the very end of the age of dinosaurs. Young ones had short horns and a small frill; both grew and changed shape as they grew up.

  8. Calm the Tyrannosaurus: The island's Tyrannosaurus rex is too strong for one. Bring friends.

    Tyrannosaurus rex had one of the strongest bites of any land animal ever, and its teeth, as long as bananas, kept growing back all its life. Its small arms were still very strong.

Dr. Fernwhistle pins your notes to her board: "A true naturalist!"

Tales of the Dragon-Keeper

Told by Seraphine the Dragon-Keeper, from level 30.

  1. Visit the Dragon-Keeper: Seraphine keeps the old dragon stories. Talk to her.

    Nearly every people on Earth told stories of dragons, from China and Japan to Greece, Scandinavia and Mexico, though they never met one. Each place imagined them differently.

  2. A pearl for the rain dragon: Deep-sea serpents carry pearls; river mussels and giant clams hold them. Bring 1 to Seraphine.

    In Chinese tales the dragon (lóng) is wise and kind, a bringer of rain for the rice fields and a sign of good luck. It is often shown chasing a glowing pearl through the clouds.

  3. Gold for a hoard: Bring 10 gold nuggets to Seraphine (gold ore, desert ruins).

    In many European stories dragons guard great piles of treasure. In the old English poem Beowulf, a dragon wakes in fury when a thief steals a single golden cup from its hoard.

  4. Acorns of the great tree: Acorn Sprouts in the Autumn Woods drop golden acorns. Bring 5 to Seraphine.

    Norse myths tell of Yggdrasil, a giant ash tree holding up the worlds, and of Nidhogg, a dragon gnawing at one of its roots in the dark far below.

  5. Feathers of the serpent: Raptors on the Primeval Isle drop feathers. Bring 3 to Seraphine.

    In ancient Mexico the Aztecs honoured Quetzalcoatl, the Feathered Serpent, a god of wind and learning; the Maya called the feathered serpent Kukulcan.

  6. Face a young drake: Calm a Caldera Drake in the Ember Wastes' craters.

    In the Greek myth of the Hydra, each head cut off grew back as two. The hero Heracles won only because his nephew Iolaus sealed each neck with fire. Remember that: heads must be sealed, not just struck.

  7. Seven scales for the Seal: The dragons of the Dragonfire Isle drop scales. Bring 7 to Seraphine.

    Japan's legend of Yamata no Orochi tells of a serpent with eight heads and eight tails. The storm god Susanoo set out eight great bowls of a strong drink, and while the monster slept he won. Wits beat many heads.

Seraphine presses the Sevenfold Seal into your hands: "The tower's gate will know you now. Go with seven friends."

The Sky Rower

Told by Rill the Otter, from level 5.

  1. Rill's big idea: Rill the Otter is at the pier. Talk to him.

    Leonardo da Vinci drew flying machines in the 1480s whose pilot beat the wings with arms and legs, like rowing.

  2. Feathers for the wing tips: Rill asks: could you please bring him 6 feathers? Chickens and bats drop them, and Vesper sells some.

    On the way down a bird's feathers close into one surface that pushes air. On the way up the wing folds a little so the air slips past.

  3. Build the Sky Rower: Build it at a workbench: planks, bamboo, cloth, feathers, rope and resin.

    A wing lifts because it pushes air down. Push air down and the air pushes you up.

  4. A first hop: Set the Sky Rower down, climb in and row with Space. Fly 30 m in one flight.

    The Wright brothers' first flight, in 1903, lasted 12 seconds and went about 37 metres.

  5. Across the bay: Fly 250 m in one flight. Row up high, then glide, and row again in short bursts.

    In 1988 the pedal-powered aircraft Daedalus flew about 115 km over the sea from Crete to Santorini, powered only by its pilot's legs.

Rill: "Every kid who flapped their arms off a porch step was right. We just needed oars!"

The Heron Count

Told by Wren's field journal, from level 2.

  1. Read Wren's field journal: Wren's camp is by the river. Press E on her notice board.

    Counting birds is real science. Since 1900, volunteers in the Christmas Bird Count have counted birds every winter, and scientists use their numbers to see how birds are doing.

  2. Watch a heron nest: Heron nests are by the water. Watch one quietly from a few steps away.

    Herons hunt by standing perfectly still in shallow water, then striking fast with their long, sharp beaks.

  3. Read two explorers' notes: Explorers' camps in the Riverlands leave notes on their crates. Read two new ones.

    Good field notes say what you saw, where and when. Scientists still read nature notebooks written hundreds of years ago.

  4. Climb Kingfisher Lookout: Find Kingfisher Lookout in the Riverlands and climb to its deck.

    Kingfishers dive head-first into water to catch fish. Japan's bullet train has a long nose shaped like a kingfisher's beak, which makes it quieter going into tunnels.

  5. Leave your count in the journal: Go back to Wren's camp and write your count in her journal (E on the board).

    When many people share what they see, even small counts add up to something big.

Wren's journal: "Forty-one herons this year! Thank you, fellow watcher."

Bones by the River

Told by Professor Pebble's notes, from level 3.

  1. Read Professor Pebble's notes: The Otterbank Fossil Dig is by the river. Press E on the notice board.

    A fossil is any trace of ancient life kept in rock: bones, shells, leaves, footprints, even burrows.

  2. Brush at the Otterbank dig: Press E on the dig, slowly and gently, until the fossil comes free.

    Fossil hunters work slowly with small brushes and picks, because old bones are fragile. Some digs take years.

  3. Brush two more fossils: Fossil digs are scattered through the Riverlands. Brush two new ones.

    Rivers carry mud and sand and lay them down in layers. Animals buried quickly in those layers are the most likely to become fossils.

  4. Dig up buried treasure: Look for disturbed earth and little tracks, then dig there with a shovel.

    Archaeologists and fossil hunters write down exactly where each find was, before they lift it: where a thing lay tells half its story.

  5. Tell a town keeper about your finds: Talk to the keeper of any town.

    The Ashmolean Museum in Oxford, opened in 1683, was one of the first museums open to the public: a place where finds are shared with everyone.

Professor Pebble's note: "A fine collection! Share it, and let everyone learn."

    People have soothed coughs with honey for thousands of years, and studies show a spoonful can calm a child's cough at night. But babies under one year old must never have honey.

  4. Race the market bell: Take your cart to the start flag on the hill, climb in (E) and roll down to the village square before the market bell.

    Rolling downhill turns height into speed. With no friction at all, a drop of 5 metres would give a speed of about 10 metres a second, whether the cart is light or heavy.

Rill: "Every kid who raced a cart down the steepest street knew it: a hill and some wheels are all the engine you need!"

The Great Ball Machine

Told by Rill the Otter, from level 12.

  1. Rill's marble run: Rill has a worried look. The river is rising upstream. Please talk to him at the pier.

    A machine where each step sets off the next is called a Rube Goldberg machine, after the American cartoonist who drew silly inventions doing simple jobs the long way round.

  2. Three chutes: Make 3 Ball Chutes at a workbench. Set them down on a river bank, end to end, starting beside where a waterwheel will turn.

    A ball speeds up down a slope because part of gravity's pull points along it. The steeper the slope, the bigger that part, so marble runs keep their tilts gentle.

  3. The seesaw: Make a Ball Seesaw and set it down with its cup under the end of your last chute.

    A seesaw is a lever. Archimedes explained levers more than 2,200 years ago, and said that with a place to stand he could move the Earth.

  4. The bell: Make a Ball Bell and stand it beside the seesaw's high end.

    A bell rings because its metal shivers after it's struck, hundreds of times a second. Each shiver pushes the air, and those waves reach your ears as sound.

  5. Set the wheel turning: Stand a Waterwheel in the river's current, about knee deep, right beside your first chute.

    People have used waterwheels for more than two thousand years. The ancient Greeks and Romans used them to grind grain into flour, with the river doing the hard work.

  6. Ring the flood warning: Press E on any part of your machine. The wheel lifts the ball, and the bell rings out for the fishers.

    Before telephones, towns rang big bells to warn everyone of floods, fires and storms. A large bell can be heard several kilometres away.

Rill: "Every kid who ran marbles across the living-room floor was an engineer. And look what yours did today!"
